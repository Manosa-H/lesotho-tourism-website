Maletsunyane Falls

A dramatic 192-metre single-drop waterfall located near Semonkong("The Place of Smoke)famous for ponny trekking and the world's highest commercial abseil. It is a single cataract on a tributary of the Orange River in Lesotho,75 miles (121km) southeast of Maseru. With a drop of 630 feet (192 meters),it is one of the world's highest waterfalls and is important to Lesotho as a tourists attraction

Activities & Attractions

• World Record Abseil: Managed by Semonkong Lodge, the site features a 204-metre (669-foot) single-drop abseil, recognized as the longest commercially operated single-drop abseil in the world. • Hiking & Trails: Visitors can walk from local visitor centers to rim viewpoints or take steep guided hikes down to the base and pool of the waterfall. • Pony Trekking: Traditional Basotho pony rides are widely available for exploring the surrounding high-altitude grasslands and valleys. • Maletsunyane Braai Festival: An annual music and social event held near the falls during the last weekend of November. • Best Time to Visit: Peak water flow occurs during and after the summer rains (November to March), while winter brings crisp alpine air and occasional snow.

Ts'ehlanyane National Park

Ts’ehlanyane National Parks is located in the Lesotho Highlands Area, which is adorned with some of the most outstanding natural assets our Kingdom of Lesotho has to offer. These treasures are protected under several statutes including the National Environment Act, No. 10 of 2008, which is based on the principles that every person in Lesotho has a right to a clean and healthy environment and that sustainable development is founded on the sound management of the environment.

Kome Caves

Perched beneath a broad sandstone overhang in the Berea district, the Ha Kome (Kome) Caves are one of Lesotho’s most evocative living heritage sites: a row of beehive-shaped mud dwellings built into a natural rock shelter and remarkably still occupied by descendants of the original families. The settlement’s distinctive rounded huts, soot-darkened ceilings and woven corral fences make for a scene that looks unchanged by time, and visiting feels less like touring a museum and more like stepping into somebody’s home.

History here is layered. Oral histories and local research link the cave village to Basia (and related) clans who sought refuge from the turbulence of the early nineteenth century, the Lifaqane/Mfecane period of warfare, displacement and famine and from bands said to practise raiding and, according to some accounts, cannibalism. Local chiefs organised the cliff dwellings as a defensible refuge; the site’s name, Ha Kome, comes from the founding Kome family. Over time the shelters were finished with plastered mud, small hearths and built-in sleeping platforms so they functioned as comfortable year-round homes.
